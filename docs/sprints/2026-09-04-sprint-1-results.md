# Sprint 1 results — GEM-Bench v0, cross-solver reproducibility, IEM ground truth v0.1

**4 September 2026 · Status: complete for the parts that fit one session; the cross-solver pass is still running and its table will be updated. All code and result files are in the `gembench` workspace archive delivered with this note.**

## 1. What was built

A small Python harness, `gembench`, with four parts: explicit media definitions applied to models from a clean state (`gembench/media.py`); dataset loaders that carry provenance and licence notes (`gembench/datasets.py`); two benchmark protocols with every threshold exposed as a parameter (`gembench/protocols/ecoli_carbon_fitness.py`, reproducing Bernstein et al. 2023, and `gembench/protocols/yeast_essential.py`, reproducing yeast-GEM's essential-gene test); metrics with gene-level bootstrap confidence intervals (`gembench/metrics.py`); benchmark cards that record dataset, model, protocol, leakage and software versions next to every result (`gembench/cards.py`); and a FROG-style report generator and comparator for cross-solver and cross-tool checks (`gembench/frog.py`). Scripts under `scripts/` run each piece and write cards, arrays and TSVs under `results/`. A separate pair of scripts parses the lab's whole-body-model IEM benchmark out of the COBRA Toolbox and links it to Orphanet, OMIM, HPO and HMDB.

Everything ran on public data reached through GitHub and PyPI, which are the only hosts the workspace can see. BiGG, BioModels, the Fitness Browser, figshare, HMDB, VMH, KEGG, UniProt and orphadata.com are not reachable from here; every dataset below was obtained from a GitHub mirror or a repository that redistributes it, and the card for each result names that copy and its commit. The machine has two cores and 7 GB of memory, which sets the scale of what ran.

## 2. E. coli knockout growth across 25 carbon sources (Bernstein et al. 2023 protocol)

The protocol was reproduced from the authors' repository (github.com/dbernste/E_coli_GEM_validation, MIT), which redistributes the Fitness Browser E. coli BW25113 RB-TnSeq data and the four BiGG models. Gene and condition counts match the authors' notebook exactly at every stage (712, 1,056, 1,148 and 1,326 genes after strain and rich-medium-essential adjustments; 27 carbon sources reduced to 25). Growth was predicted with GLPK, single-gene deletions per carbon source, growth threshold 0.001, carbon uptake −10, base M9 components unlimited.

| Model | AUC-PR, Bernstein convention [95% CI] | AUC-PR, standard convention | MCC | Balanced accuracy | Genes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| iJR904 (2003) | 0.659 [0.555, 0.755] | 0.590 | 0.629 | 0.774 | 712 |
| iAF1260 (2007) | 0.638 [0.533, 0.730] | 0.518 | 0.593 | 0.750 | 1,056 |
| iJO1366 (2011) | 0.621 [0.522, 0.706] | 0.467 | 0.578 | 0.751 | 1,148 |
| iML1515 (2017) | 0.613 [0.512, 0.709] | 0.479 | 0.586 | 0.751 | 1,326 |
| iML1515 with the authors' corrections | 0.764 [0.685, 0.831] | 0.567 | 0.664 | 0.792 | 1,326 |

The Bernstein convention treats the model's no-growth call as the label and experimental fitness as the score, which is how the paper avoids thresholding the experimental data; the standard convention treats fitness below −2 as the label and the model's growth as the score. Both are in every card, together with per-carbon-source values.

Four things are worth Tim's attention. First, the reproduction recovers the paper's qualitative story: accuracy falls monotonically across the four model generations as coded (0.66 to 0.61) and the corrected iML1515 recovers to 0.76. Second, the bootstrap intervals are about ±0.10 wide, so the decline across the four versions is not statistically distinguishable at this sample size, whereas the effect of the corrections is; nothing in the original analysis reports uncertainty, and this is the first time these numbers carry it. Third, the notebook's comment says sucrose and mannitol are excluded but the code excludes BiGG id `man`, which is D-mannose; the paper text says mannitol. Running the mannitol-excluded variant gives 0.612 for iML1515 against 0.613 as coded, so the discrepancy does not change the conclusion, but it is a documented code-versus-text divergence. Fourth, the published article is reported (by the independent reviewer of the roadmap, who read the full text) to state AUC-PR values around 0.81 rising to 0.85, whereas the preprint reported roughly 0.65 rising to 0.72 and this reproduction gives 0.61 rising to 0.76 from the public code and data. The most likely explanations are a change of metric convention or of data or model version between preprint and publication; this should be reconciled with the authors before GEM-Bench quotes their number as its reference, and it is itself a small illustration of why a shared executable benchmark is needed.

Per carbon source, iML1515's worst conditions are glycerol (0.51), D-serine (0.54), acetate (0.55), glycolate (0.56) and α-ketoglutarate (0.56); the best are glucose-6-phosphate (0.71), D-glucuronate (0.69), glucosamine (0.68), galactose (0.67) and galacturonate (0.67). Ten genes were dropped as essential in an unrestricted medium. The whole run takes about two minutes per model on this machine.

## 3. Yeast single-gene deletion viability (yeast-GEM protocol)

yeast-GEM 9.1.1 (github.com/SysBioChalmers/yeast-GEM, main branch) was scored against the Stanford deletion collection lists shipped in the repository, using the Kennedy synthetic complete medium and the 1e-6 growth-ratio tolerance of the repository's own test. The result reproduces the repository's stored test output exactly: 933 true positives, 65 true negatives, 94 false positives, 15 false negatives over 1,107 verified ORFs; accuracy 0.902 [0.883, 0.918]; MCC 0.532 [0.451, 0.604]. The gap between accuracy and MCC is the class-imbalance point from the roadmap made concrete: only 159 of the 1,107 evaluated genes are inviable, the model predicts inviability for just 80 genes in total, and 65 of those 80 are right. The leakage card notes that this test has been part of yeast-GEM's curation regression suite since yeast7, so it is an in-sample number.

## 4. Cross-solver reproducibility (FROG-style)

`gembench/frog.py` computes the FROG quantities (objective, flux variability at the optimum, single gene deletions, single reaction deletions) for a model as shipped under a chosen solver, writes them as TSV fixtures, and compares two fixture sets with absolute and relative tolerances and growth-call disagreement counts. The fixture format is deliberately plain so that MATLAB Toolbox output can be dropped in for the same comparison once MATLAB is available. The pass over the model set (four E. coli models, yeast-GEM, Human-GEM 2.0, Recon3D, iMM904, Salmonella STM_v1.0, iND750, iIT341, the E. coli core model, fourteen AGORA-type microbial models from the Toolbox's test set and twenty-eight EMBL GEMs) compares GLPK with HiGHS (through COBRApy's hybrid interface). It is still running because HiGHS without warm starts takes about fifteen times longer per LP than GLPK in COBRApy; the models finished so far (iJR904, iAF1260, iJO1366) agree on objective, deletions and FVA to within 1e-6. The divergence table will be appended to this note when the pass completes.

## 5. IEM biomarker ground truth v0.1

The lab's whole-body-model benchmark (57 IEMs, 252 biofluid-specific biomarkers in Thiele et al. 2020) exists as code, not as a table: the COBRA Toolbox script `runIEM_HH.m` encodes each IEM as a gene id, a set of reaction patterns to knock out and a list of biomarker exchange or demand reactions with an expected direction. `scripts/parse_iem_ground_truth.py` turns it into a tuple table with exact provenance (repository commit and line numbers): 63 `checkIEM_WBM` calls, 62 abbreviations, 279 (IEM, metabolite, model compartment, direction) tuples, 135 tested in urine, 136 in blood, 7 in CSF; 240 expected increases and 39 decreases. The paper counts 57 IEMs and 252 biomarkers, so the script is a superset and the reconciliation against the paper's supplementary table is an open item.

`scripts/link_iem_ground_truth.py` then links the table outward using only licence-clean sources reached through GitHub: Human-GEM 2.0's gene table for Entrez-to-symbol mapping, Orphanet's `product6` (genes), `product1` (cross-references) and `product4` (HPO phenotypes) from the Orphadata GitHub mirror (CC-BY-4.0, June 2026), the HPO release of 1 September 2026, and Recon3D's metabolite annotations for HMDB, ChEBI and KEGG identifiers. Of the 63 IEM calls, 61 map to a gene symbol, 58 to an Orphanet disorder (chosen by name similarity among the causal gene's disorders, with four low-confidence matches flagged), 57 to OMIM numbers, and 36 to disorders that carry HPO metabolite-concentration terms. One hundred of the 125 distinct VMH metabolites received an HMDB identifier; the 25 without (mostly acylcarnitines, steroid intermediates and glycine conjugates) are listed for manual mapping.

The HPO cross-check is the first independent test of the lab's table. Parsing HPO term names into (metabolite, biofluid, direction) with documented heuristics, 39 of the 279 tuples are corroborated exactly, 11 are corroborated for the same metabolite and direction in a different biofluid (typically the model tests urine where HPO records blood, or vice versa), 106 are not among the disorder's HPO metabolite terms, 122 belong to disorders with no HPO metabolite-concentration terms at all, and one is a direct conflict: for glycogen storage disease type VI (Hers disease) the script expects blood glucose to be increased, while HPO lists hypoglycemia as very frequent. That row should be reviewed by the lab. In the other direction, 99 HPO-derived tuples for these disorders are absent from the table (hyperammonemia in argininemia and CPS1 deficiency, hypoargininemia in CPS1 deficiency, reduced circulating creatine in AGAT deficiency, and so on); they are the natural first extension set, each traceable to an HPO annotation with frequency. Three gene ids in the script headers look mistyped and are flagged rather than corrected: 1181.1 for essential pentosuria (DCXR is Entrez 51181), 1538.1 for the STAR block (STAR is 6770), and 4967.1 used for both α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase deficiency (OGDH, correct) and adenylosuccinase deficiency (ADSL is 158).

The table is `results/iem_ground_truth/iem_biomarkers_v0.1_linked.tsv`; the HPO-derived candidates are `hpo_metabolite_tuples_not_in_lab_table.tsv`; counts and caveats are in `linking_report.json` and `provenance.json`.

## 7. Proposed Sprint 2

Three pieces, in order. First, whole-body models in Python: the Toolbox's test-model repository ships Harvey and Harvetta 1.03d as MATLAB files with their coupling constraints; loading them, adding the coupling constraints that COBRApy ignores, and solving with HiGHS and GLPK will show whether open solvers can handle the lab's flagship models at all, and gives the oracle test for the Python whole-body capability that WS3 now has to build itself. Second, the IEM protocol in Python on those models, scored against the v0.1 table, which is the first step toward reproducing the 85 percent figure outside MATLAB and then toward per-disease precision and recall. Third, the propose-then-verify loop v0 on MACAW flags in iML1515 with the E. coli benchmark as the regression gate. In parallel, two data requests that only Tim can unblock: the Fitness Browser per-organism tables for gut bacteria that also have AGORA2 reconstructions (Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron VPI-5482 first), which would give the benchmark its lab-relevant organisms, and MATLAB access for the Toolbox side of the conformance suite.
